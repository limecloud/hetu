# Plan an outline and chapters

## Start with what you need

Outline the level needed for the current problem. A scene needs no complete book plan; drafting before outlining is valid. Keep alternatives distinct from settled events.

An outline helps you discuss where a story could go and leaves decisions you can continue from later. It can begin with one question; every chapter does not need to be planned on the first day.

Ask your Agent to compare directions and organize proposals. You decide which to pursue. Keep possible developments separate from facts already established in the manuscript.

## Describe your interests and limits

Characters, circumstances, relationships and the feeling you want often help more than a long list of genre labels.

> I want to write about a watchmaker returning home to find her father. An old clock carries tomorrow's voices, but cannot freely change the past. Focus on their relationship and life in the town, without a world-saving plot. Propose two story directions, each with an opening, central conflict and the cost of its ending.

Provide existing passages as fixed references. Leave uncertain details as open questions instead of letting the Agent treat guesses as established lore.

For a new book's title and profile, see [Plan a book with Codex](codex-first-task.md).

## Compare directions before expanding

First consider whether the character's situation invites further questions, whether the conflict can sustain the story and whether the ending answers the opening.

You can keep one proposal's central relationship and reject its mystery explanation. Ask the Agent to preserve those choices before expanding, so each discussion builds on your decisions.

Make “the second version is better” more precise: “Keep the neighbors helping each other. Let the daughter make the final choice rather than introducing someone to explain the secret.”

## Move from story direction to the next chapter

| Level | Main question | What to review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Story direction | Why tell this story? | Desire, resistance and cost |
| Overall outline | Where does it begin and end? | Causality, relationships and resolution |
| Volume outline | What changes in this phase? | Its goal and resulting situation |
| Chapter outline | What happens next? | Action, information, a turn and continuation |

Structure should serve the story. Chapters need not have identical lengths, and a planning template should not force an early revelation.

Ask for enough detail to discuss the next chapter: what the character knows, what she wants, what she does and what changes by the end. Then read a full reference draft; see [Chapters and writing](writing.md).

## Keep room to change and preserve confirmed facts

An outline can change, and written chapters can change too. Ask the Agent to identify affected plans and retain the previous proposal for comparison.

If you decide the father never left town, revisit the search route, witnesses and misleading clues. First list the relevant material rather than rewriting every chapter automatically.

You confirm formal story facts. See [Maintain your story bible](story-bible.md). Works using a similar character do not automatically share their entire canon.

## Make the question smaller when you are stuck

If judging the whole book is difficult, ask: “Should the daughter trust her father more or suspect him more by the end of this chapter? Try one direction for each.”

Compare the consequences and continue one, or reject both. The useful outcome is a decision that helps you write, rather than an impressive outline you do not want to follow.

For revisions, keep the original proposal and precise feedback; see [Give feedback and revise](feedback.md).
