## You know the character. Your collaborator needs a scene.

Mara hears her missing brother on a radio that has not worked in years. In the novel, the next page can move through seven years of grief. On screen, a collaborator needs to know what happens in the room.

Does she reach for the dial? Say his name? Pretend she heard nothing? What does the audience learn before the lighthouse goes dark?

An independent author can know every answer about a character and still find the first screenplay page difficult. A new medium asks different questions. A small team then has to turn those answers into material another person can use.

## One passage, several creative decisions

Consider this original manuscript example:

> Mara knew the voice on the broken radio. She kept her hand away from the dial. Her brother had been missing for seven years. Then the lighthouse went dark.

A possible adaptation adds visible action and a new line:

> Mara reaches for the radio. A familiar voice crackles through the static. Her hand freezes. The beam outside cuts out.
>
> MARA: Say my name again.

The new line changes the scene. She is testing the voice, and perhaps hoping it will pass the test. Silence would make a different promise. Neither choice follows automatically from shortening the prose.

Then come production questions. Which radio? Is its casing already cracked? What can Mara see after the power fails? If the next episode starts in the same room, which references should a collaborator use? These details turn an interesting draft into work that can continue.

## Three kinds of work connect a manuscript to production

| Starting material | A useful next form | The question people must answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A character’s inner voice | Action, dialogue and sound | What should the audience infer? |
| A sequence of chapters | Scenes and episode turns | Where should tension build, change or pause? |
| A fictional world | Character, location and prop references | Which details need to survive the next revision? |

An external Agent can help explore each step: propose three openings, test a quieter line or list references for a scene. Someone still has to judge whether the result belongs to this story. Useful software keeps the original nearby, the alternatives separate and the accepted choices identifiable.

## A small team needs a shared version of “the current draft”

Imagine revising Mara’s radio from a portable set to a fixed maritime receiver. The new scene may be better, but yesterday’s prop reference now describes the wrong object. A new conversation with an Agent may still use that old reference.

“Keep it consistent” is a useful intention. A named source revision and a clear selection are much more useful to the next collaborator.

## Start with a scene you would want someone to perform

Choose a passage you have the rights to adapt. Give the character an immediate goal and an obstacle. Write one scene, compare it to the source, and keep the choices that express your intent. Then note the references a collaborator would need.

You now have something specific to discuss. One strong scene can be a better starting point than a folder full of unreviewed episodes.
